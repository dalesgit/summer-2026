## Opening 

Did anyone notice that we had an election this last week? When I first saw the schedule about who is to preach and celebrate on this day I thought to myself is there some kind of conspiracy? It turns out that I was scheduled to preach on the day after election day two years ago.

it’s amazing to think that anyone would remember what I preach that day. But I happen to know that several of you remember my sermon on that day. It included a kind of experiment when I dragged yarn all up and down the center aisle. I was trying to illustrate the importance of putting things into perspective. Human beings have only been a part of God‘s creation, at least in the manifestation on this planet, for only a tiny fraction of the time that God has been about the business of creating the universe.

As it turns out, I was also preaching the Sunday after Donald Trump was elected for the first time. It wasn’t around here and I doubt anybody remembers it except for my wife. But for me, it was a very memorable day. I talked about the history of the church of the 20th century. I talked about how the last few weeks of the church year. Have a thematic focus on end times. ## I’ve changed my mind about things over the years.

in my early years of preaching, I pretty studiously tried to unpack the meaning of the text from the Bible, the scripture readings of the day, and try to draw lessons. I talked briefly the last time I preached a month ago about how I am learning to reverse the process. I don’t think that we need to be conscious and aware of where we are today and scripture and tradition can teach us how to live in times like these.

there are a couple of things that I have concluded for myself over the years. Things that I am inclined to say, I know. One of them is that God always meets us where we are. We bumped into the sacred, usually at surprising times, times that catch us unawares, while we are busy, tending to the lives we’re trying to live.

The Bible and the tradition gave us manifold examples of this kind of thing. Abraham was living a nice ordinary life in the place. He called home. Then he heard God and God said I want you to leave all this behind. The result was that he became the father of many nations, the father of several different religions. He had to be shaken out of the comfort of his home.

Many centuries later, a man named Anthony looked around his culture, it was a thoroughly Christian culture around the 300s, and he was horrified by what he saw. God shook him from inside out and sent him off to live in the desert of Egypt and he became the inspiration for countless men and women up to the present day.

some centuries later, Francis looked around at his culture, and he was horrified like Anthony had been. He testified and administered in God‘s name to the present reality that he found himself in. They debated amongst themselves whether he was for real or not. Some rejected his rejection of the wealth and opulence around him. Others found inspiration up to the present day.

some centuries later, Dorothy Day looked at her Christian culture had felt convicted. The Christian culture around me, she thought, looks nothing like the values that Jesus himself taught in the beatitudes. She spent the last many decades of her life, offering herself and her testimony, to the poor and the least, to the sick and the suffering.

these examples are rich, of course, beyond measure. But one thing I think they illustrate is the importance of confronting what is around us, Day by Day, and being open to God‘s conviction, God‘s command, God‘s instruction.

Only then is it really possible to hear what scripture has to tell us. scripture provides nurture and direction only after we engage the world in which we actually live. 


## Who are we?

when I look out at you.  What do I see? To begin with I see a fair amount of diversity. There are some young people. a preponderance of old people. 

One of the things I learned many years ago is that when I look out at you there is one thing I can fairly reliably predict. Each of you has come through those doors looking for something. There are too many other options available to you that choosing to come here on a Sunday morning has to be deliberate.

you come here for many different places. You’re probably not native to Florida. You speak English. You’re relatively wealthy, certainly by international standards.

some of you are happy some of you are sad. Some of you have lost something or anticipate a loss. Some of you are working at new beginnings. Some of you are fearful. Some of you are bold. But all of you are looking for something to satisfy, to comfort, to put into perspective, whatever it is, you’re living through.

I put that together with another thing that I have learned over the years. I’ve said it here before, because I think it’s important. It’s an inspiration that came to me just a handful of years ago. I think it’s the case that every one of us in this room thinks that we’re right.

I don’t mean that we’re bullheaded about it. Perhaps we’re quite convinced that we don’t know anything worthwhile. But we know it, nevertheless, and if I disagree with you, you will find reasons to counter me. 

each of us is living in the skin that we have been given, with our experience, our comprehension, our abilities, such as they are.

## Scripture and Tradition

there are so many things that speak and evoke for me a sense of the end times. Until very very recently, I could not have imagined the Arctic melting. What seemed secure and foundational is now shaken.

Paul was literally writing to Thessalonians who were grieving. They were grieving the death of their friends and family members. Paul writes to them with a vision of new life and the strength to encourage one another.

Talking in stories, as Jesus is wont to do, he gives us the message to keep alert, keep awake, because you do not know the day or the hour. We will hear that kind of message as advent begin begins in the first weeks of December.


## whatever shall we do with it?

now we can turn to the scripture and find echoes and reflections of our experience. The scripture can open up for us possibilities and understandings.

tradition also can give us possibilities and new understandings. The church year itself is circular not linear. Sacred time doesn’t so much have beginnings and endings the way our ordinary common time does. We think of time is something long ago or hope some dreams for what we want to come true in the future.

We are just weeks away from the end of the church year. That’s why the readings for the last few weeks of the year speak in one way or another about 10 times. There are crises that must be met and prepared for. But in the wisdom we have inherited, the Church year knows that endings turn into beginnings. advent begins in three weeks.

last week, father Peter in his sermon, evoked the question, what happens to us after we die? He did this in the context of All Saints, and was trying to illustrate that the Saints are still with us. There are endings, but that’s not the whole story. 

in a reflection on all souls day yesterday, we read a reflection that said something similar: at every funeral there are two preachers. One of them is death, but the other is Hope and the resurrection. 

The prayer book says something similar about funerals and death.

*The liturgy for the dead is an Easter liturgy. It finds all meaning in the resurrection. Because Jesus was raised from the dead, we too, shall be raised.*

*The liturgy, therefore, is characterized by joy, in the certainty that "neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord."*

*This joy, however, does not make human grief unchristian. The very love we have for each other in Christ brings deep sorrow when we are parted by death. Jesus himself wept at the grave of his friend. So, while we rejoice that one we love has entered into the nearer presence of our Lord, we sorrow in sympathy with those who mourn. 

(p. 507)*

## Elections are like funerals

I think election day is experienced something like a funeral. Funerals can be full of partying. An Irish wake, for example. I have experienced the way in which funerals bring families together in ways nothing else can. At the same time, something is over. Hopes and dreams are pushed out of reach.
