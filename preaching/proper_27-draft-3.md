## Opening 

Did anyone notice that we had an election this last week? When I first saw the schedule about who is to preach and celebrate on this day I thought to myself is there some kind of conspiracy? It turns out that I was scheduled to preach on the day after election day two years ago.

It’s amazing to think that anyone would remember what I preach that day. But I happen to know that several of you remember my sermon on that day. It included a kind of experiment when I dragged yarn all up and down the center aisle. I was trying to illustrate the importance of putting things into perspective. Human beings have only been a part of God‘s creation, at least in the manifestation on this planet, for only a tiny fraction of the time that God has been about the business of creating the universe.

As it turns out, I was also preaching the Sunday after Donald Trump was elected for the first time. It wasn’t around here and I doubt anybody remembers it except for my wife. But for me, it was a memorable day. I talked about the history of the church of the 20th century. I talked about how the last few weeks of the church year have a thematic focus on end times. I pose some questions that I think about all the time:

1. what would Jesus do?
2. How can we be responsible for God‘s reputation in times like this?

I guess that’s the kind of thing that elections do to me. I try to ask big questions.
## I’ve changed my mind about things over the years.

In my early years of preaching, I pretty studiously tried to unpack the meaning of the text from the Bible first -- the scripture readings of the day -- and then try to draw lessons from them. I talked briefly the last time I preached a month ago about how I am learning to reverse the process. I think that we need to be conscious and aware of where we are today  our lived experience. Then, as a second step, turn to scripture and tradition so that they can teach us how to live in times like these.

The building blocks of this way of thinking about preaching comes from a basic principal of the Anglican way of doing things. Just this past week we observed in the calendar of saints, one of the pillars of the early days of the Anglican tradition.

Richard Hooker lived in the 1500s. responding to the controversies of his day, he developed principles for discerning what God is doing in the midst of all that we are experiencing in the present. He saw the authority for discerning the gospel for us in today’s world as being based on three pillars: scripture, tradition, and experience.

Trying to apply these principles, there are a couple of things that I have concluded for myself over the years. Things that I am inclined to say, I know that!! One of them is that God always meets us where we are. Not what we used to be and not what we hope for. God meets us right here in the mess of where we are.  We bump into the sacred, usually at surprising times, times that catch us unawares, while we are busy, tending to the lives we’re trying to live.

The Bible and the tradition give us manifold examples of this kind of thing. Abraham was living a nice ordinary life in the place he called home. Then he heard God and God said I want you to leave all this behind. The result was that he became the father of many nations, the father of several different religions. He had to be shaken out of the comfort of his home.

Many centuries later, a man named Anthony looked around his culture, it was a thoroughly Christian culture around the 300s, and he was horrified by what he saw. God shook him from inside out and sent him off to live in the desert of Egypt and he became the inspiration for countless men and women up to the present day.

Some centuries later, Francis looked around at his culture, and he was horrified like Anthony had been. He testified and administered in God‘s name to the present reality that he found himself in. They debated amongst themselves whether he was for real or not. Some rejected his rejection of the wealth and opulence around him. Others found inspiration up to the present day.

Some centuries later, Dorothy Day looked at her Christian culture had felt convicted. The Christian culture around me, she thought, looks nothing like the values that Jesus himself taught in the beatitudes. She spent the last many decades of her life, offering herself and her testimony, to the poor and the least, to the sick and the suffering.

These examples are rich, of course, beyond measure. But one thing I think they illustrate is the importance of confronting what is around us, Day by Day, and being open to God‘s conviction, God‘s command, God‘s instruction.

Only then is it really possible to hear what scripture has to tell us. Scripture provides nurture and direction only after we engage the world in which we actually live. 
## Who are we here and now?

When I look out at you.  What do I see? To begin with, I see some diversity. There are some young people and lots of older people. But mostly, I see people who are pretty similar too one another. 

One of the things I learned many years ago is that when I look out at a congregation gathered before me, there is one thing I can fairly reliably predict. Each of you has come through those doors looking for something. You are searching for something. Hoping for something. There are too many other options available to you that choosing to come here on a Sunday morning has to be deliberate.

You come here from many different places. You’re probably not native to Florida. You speak English. You’re relatively wealthy, certainly by international standards.

Some of you are happy some of you are sad. Some of you have lost something or anticipate a loss. Some of you are working at new beginnings. Some of you are fearful. Some of you are bold. But all of you are looking for something to satisfy, to comfort, to put into perspective, whatever it is, you’re living through.

But that description barely scratches the surface of the wider and deeper reality that we find ourselves in today.

I put observation together with another thing that I have learned over the years. I’ve said it here before, because I think it’s important. It’s an insight that came to me just a handful of years ago. I am usually very cautious about saying that something is universally the case.But I think that every one of us in this room thinks that we’re right.

I don’t mean that we’re bullheaded about it. Perhaps we’re quite convinced that we don’t know anything worthwhile. But we know it, nevertheless, and if I disagree with you, you will find reasons to counter me. 

We think we’re right because everything we have experienced and learned in the course of our life culminates in what we think we know today.

Each of us is living in the skin that we have been given, with our experience, our comprehension, our abilities, such as they are.

That is what God has to work with.
## Scripture Illuminates

There are so many things about the world we are living in that speak and evoke for me a sense of the end times. Until very very recently, I could not have imagined the Arctic melting. What seemed secure and foundational is now shaken.

Our readings from scripture today also reflect a keen experience of the end times approaching. 

Amos, the prophet uses the phrase day of the Lord to describe this. "is not the day of the Lord darkness, not light and gloom with no brightness in it?" "Let just roll down like waters and righteousness like an overflowing stream." 

Paul was literally writing to Thessalonians who were grieving. They were grieving the death of their friends and family members. Paul writes to them with a vision of new life and the strength to encourage one another.

Talking in stories, as Jesus is wont to do, he gives us the message to keep alert, keep awake, because you do not know the day or the hour. The foolish and wise bridesmaids are a symbol of the two kinds of people facing the end times. Those were prepared, and those who are not. We will hear that kind of message as advent begin begins in the first weeks of December.

Scripture, then, helps us to find echoes and reflections of our own experience. It opens up for us possibilities and understandings. 

## Tradition illuminates

Tradition can also give us possibilities and new understandings. 

The church year itself is circular not linear. Sacred time doesn’t so much have beginnings and endings the way our ordinary common time does. We think of time as something long ago or hoped for in dreams for what we want to come true in the future. 

Ordinary time is linear. Sacred time is circular. The beginning and the ending are found at the same place in a circle.

We are just weeks away from the end of the church year. That’s why the readings for the last few weeks of the year speak in one way or another about the **end times**. There are crises that must be met and prepared for. But in the wisdom we have inherited, the Church year knows that endings turn into beginnings. Advent begins in three weeks.

In Fr. Peter's sermon last week, he evoked the question, "What happens to us after we die?" He did this in the context of **All Saints**, and was trying to illustrate that the **Saints** are still with us. They may be gone, but they are also present. 

Scripture, Tradition, and Experience all teach us that God is present to us in paradox. There are endings, but that’s not the whole story. 

In a reflection on **All Soul's** day – i.e. the day after **All Saints** day, at home we read a reflection that said something similar to what Fr. Peter was getting at. We read that, "… at every funeral there are two preachers. One of them is death, but the other is Hope and the resurrection."

The prayer book says something similar about funerals and death. There is grief but also hope.

> (p. 507) *The liturgy for the dead is an Easter liturgy. It finds all meaning in the resurrection. Because Jesus was raised from the dead, we too, shall be raised.*
>
> *The liturgy, therefore, is characterized by joy, in the certainty that "neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord."*
>
> *This joy, however, does not make human grief unchristian. The very love we have for each other in Christ brings deep sorrow when we are parted by death. Jesus himself wept at the grave of his friend. So, while we rejoice that one we love has entered into the nearer presence of our Lord, we sorrow in sympathy with those who mourn. *

“In my beginning is my end,” said a great poet. God meets us where we live **in paradox**. Life and death, beginnings and endings, are all a part of God's creation.  

## Elections are like funerals

Election day for me is a perfect opportunity to think about endings and beginnings. They take the outward appearance of victories and defeats. I think elections are something like funerals. They mark endings and beginnings, yes, but funerals, afterall, sad though they obviously are, can be full of partying as well. An Irish wake, for example. Families come together around funerals. At funerals we look for connections when what we feel around us is separations. 

In separation, cut off from one another, our hopes and dreams are pushed out of reach. Elections are invitations to discover what binds us together when we feel the separations.

Elections invite us to take the long view. There are moments of intense, victory, or defeat, but elections are a bet on a future that we do not know.

> Now we see a reflection in a mirror; then we will see face-to-face. Now I know partially, but then I will know completely in the same way that I have been completely known. (1 Co. 13)

The approaching end of the church year can encourage us when taking that long view to recognize that our end is our beginning. Time is not one thing after another, on and on and on … Time is circular from the perspective of eternity. The sacred -- God -- works in circular time, not linear. 

Elections are like the life that we live. One day we face enormous crossroads. Decisions are right or wrong and we suffer consequences. But that's not the end of the story. Elections are symbols that the victory of God counts for everything. 

Thanks be to God.
