## Opening

It’s amazing to me to think that just a month ago I was standing behind a church in Seydisfjordur, Iceland. A little tiny village of a little over 600 people on the north-eastern side of the island nation. Until fairly recently it was the major town on the island being a good distribution point for the fishing which was the main business of the population.

I was looking down on an archaeological dig from a few years ago that had uncovered a house built around the time of the earliest Viking settlement ca. 1,000 years ago.

I felt a sense of connection to folks long, long ago. At that point the Vikings were not yet Christianized. That would come in a couple of centuries. I felt somehow related in ways I can’t explain.

A few days later we woke up on the ship hoping to be able to see a total eclipse of the sun. For many there was a sense of disappointment as we were mostly covered with clouds during the actual event. But not me. I caught some glimpses of the moon as it moved in between us and the sun -- a shadow moving across the sun, if you will. I felt a sense of connection to the universe itself. Stars, planets, -- so much bigger than me. Consider that just a few hundred years ago it was a hotly contested notion whether we lived in a solar system with the sun in the center of things.

> Come to think of it, there are still some who might dispute that kind of basic reality. Those who would claim to be speaking for Christianity while reading the book of Genesis literally. Such folks miss out on so much. They miss the profound sense of connection that I felt then -- a kind of echo of my sense that we are all of us just made of bits of stardust.

We live in a strange reality these days.

## It’s all energy and relationships

My travels gave me a sense of **connection** to the universe. You’ve heard from me and others that “**we are stardust**”. That’s what that means. I knew someone who was at Woodstock in 1969. I was traveling in Europe that summer. Joni Mitchell wrote the song by that name, and in it we hear the words: "We are stardust, We are golden / We are billion-year-old carbon."

That's my generation. It may not be your background, and the generations after us *Boomers* have different data to work with. But the truth is that when we think so highly of ourselves, when we think that we're special -- whoever we are -- and we all think that way -- when we exalt ourselves at others' expense -- we have lost sight of the fact that we are all related. We are all part of the same God-made stuff.

When I heard the Gaelic music in Nova Scotia I made connections with my past. When I saw videos of the fishing business in Iceland during the early 1950's, I saw photos of young people who might well have been me. 

Sailing on a ship with several thousand folks is a remarkable experience. There are two classes of folks. The guests are on decks 1-9. Some of those guests thinj it's a sign of rank how high your cabin is. Then there is the staff. They live on the decks below #1. Servants and served. But for me the overarching experience was that all of us, servant and served alike, we all on the same ship. 

We're all connected.

## Things are amiss

Perspective provides an ability to see things in 3D. Where before we only saw in black and white. When a different perspective arises, our world can come alive with new perceptions and new insights.

In ancient times a total solar eclipse was a sign from the universe (the gods?) that had the power to bring down a mighty empire. It could be a powerful enough sign that whole populations might move from one place to another. Immigrants all.

Today it’s a photo-op. Or a missed photo-op as the case may be. Seeing the world from a different perspective allowed me to come home with new insights. When I looked around I saw signs that things were amiss. For me it was more obvious as we traveled around the north Atlantic, following the path that Norse sailors had sailed a thousand years ago. It seemed obvious that things were going badly wrong.

> If we have eyes to see and ears to hear ... But it appears more and more that we don’t have eyes and ears, because we have covered them up.

As for myself, anticipating coming home to Palm Harbor, things looked a mess. Some of you may see it differently, and that's ok. I remember fondly the time that I put a similar question in a sermon in South Carolina. One old gentleman walked up to me after the service and said that he very much disagreed with me. I valued his comments more than most. But I do see things having gone terribly awry in the world around me, and I'm uncertain what to do about it.

## Settings of old narratives

The readings from the Bible that we’ve heard this morning, each of them in their own way, reflect how the world around them had gone awry.

Jonah, such a strange figure, and a strange little book bundled with all the other major and minor prophets. We’re familiar with the major outlines: Jonah being contacted by God with a mission. Jonah saying in response, "Is there somebody else up there?" And then he trie to run away. Only to be rescued by God and seemingly reluctantly heading off to his assignment, preaching repentance to the Ninevites. They repented and God relented, much to Jonah’s irritation. That’s where we pick it up in today’s reading.  God looks down, sees terrible mischief going on. God assigned someone to warn the offenders, and they are reluctant to follow the voice of God.

Jesus tells a parable to his followers many centuries later. He wants them to become prophetic witnesses to God‘s Kingdom. They are reluctant. 

> Well maybe that’s an ongoing theme?

So in a common pattern, Jesus tells them a story, a parable. It’s a parable about workers and how the manager seems to show no partiality. How might we tell that in today’s world? In a world where workers are generally exploited. Where the historical representatives of workers, the unions, are virtually outlawed.  

How do we tell a parable in today’s setting where our expectations of what is appropriate and fair are overthrown by God‘s overwhelming compensation?

If God were to speak to someone in our midst, how would we recognize it? Who could command enough respect from the folks of Palm Harbor in Florida that we would drop everything and try to fulfill our assignment?

## Reluctant Prophets

Given the diagnosis, where do we turn? Who do we look to for guidance and direction? Given that we live in a society that is strongly influenced by a sense of “ain’t nobody gonna tell me what to do” Who or where might we find the authority, the motivation to follow God‘s mission?

The old workhorses seem less and less capable. Theologies written for a world of centuries ago seem odd living in a world where all of our accumulated knowledge can multiply in days not centuries? The old authorities hold less and less for us. To whom do we turn?

Yes we read from the Bible -- but how many of us make life decisions on the basis that reading? We hear plenty of stories from of old to that effect. But today? 

An old adage puts it: "Want to know where your priorities are? Look at your checkbook."

Of course that adage needs to be reworked since checkbooks are obsolete. But the point is very well taken, "Follow the money."

The Ninevites heard God in Jonah's voice, even though he acted the coward and smelled fishy. Jesus's disciples heard the voice of authority in what today we might call a union organiser.

If God were to speak to one in our midst, how would we recognize it?

## Response

I'm full of questions. Any of you who know me will know that's normal for me. I will make some suggestions in response to my questions. But who am I that you would pay any attention to me?

1. Listen in unexpected ways. Listen in the silence. Listen in the shower. Those are two ways I listen. But I've heard God in clouds. In a bird flying out of a woman's hair. I've seen God in 3 Inuit children playing in the water at the beach. (The water had ice flowing in it.) 
2. Pay attention precisely to those who are unlike you. That's why getting outside of our comfort zones is so important. If the message we get sounds too much like ourselves -- we might well have missed it.
3. Respect those who have gone before and honor and protect those who will come after. The past has much to teach us -- but it is often a dead end for us. What we do today is not really for our comfort and pleaseure. It is for the generation to come. (Shema, yisrael, adonai eloheni, adonai echad. *You shall love the L-rd your [G‑d](https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/433240/jewish/God.htm "God in Judaism") with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might. And these words which I command you today shall be upon your heart. You shall teach them thoroughly to your children, and you shall speak of them when you sit in your house and when you walk on the road, when you lie down and when you rise.*)

I could go on. But it's not about me. "Only you can prevent forest fires", Smokey said to us when we were growing up.

Identify where you have heard the prophets among you speaking.

Pay attention.

Bow down in awe.
