## Opening

It’s amazing to me to think that just a month ago I was standing behind a church in Seydisfjordur, Iceland. A tiny village of a little over 600 people on the north-eastern side of the island nation. Until fairly recently it was a major town on the island, being a good distribution point for the fishing which was the main business of the population.

I was looking down on an archaeological dig from a few years ago that had uncovered a house built around the time of the earliest Viking settlement ca. 1,000 years ago.

I felt a sense of connection to folks long, long ago. I felt somehow related in ways I can’t explain. At that point the Vikings were not yet Christianized, for that would come in a couple of centuries. 

A few days later we woke up on the ship hoping to be able to see a total eclipse of the sun. For many there was a sense of disappointment as we were mostly covered with clouds during the actual event. 

But not me. I caught some glimpses of the moon as it moved in between us and the sun -- a shadow moving across the sun, if you will. I felt a sense of connection to the universe itself. Stars, planets, -- so much bigger than me. Consider that just a few hundred years ago it was a hotly contested notion whether we lived in a solar system with the sun in the center of things.

Come to think of it, there are probably still some who might dispute that kind of basic reality. Those who would claim to be speaking for Christianity while reading the book of Genesis literally. _Such folks miss out on so much._ They miss the profound sense of connection that I felt then -- a kind of echo of my sense that we are all of us just made of bits of stardust.

We live in a strange reality these days.

## It’s all energy and relationships

My travels gave me a sense of **connection** to the universe. You’ve heard from me and others that “**we are stardust**”. That’s what that means. Joni Mitchell wrote the song that includes the line. "We are stardust, We are golden We are billion-year-old carbon And we've got to get ourselves back to the garden "

 Mitchell wrote it and it was made famous by _Crosby, Stills and Nash._ Their music may not be your cup of tea, but the thing is, quite literally, the creator of the universe made all there is out of a basic set of building blocks. We are part of it and those building blocks move throughout the universe. The truth is that when I think too highly of myself, when I think that I'm special -- we all think that way -- when I exalt myself at others' expense -- I have lost sight of the fact that we are all related. We are all part of the same God-made stuff.

When I heard the Gaelic music in Nova Scotia, I made connections with my past. When I saw videos of the fishing business in Iceland during the early 1950's, I saw photos of young people who might well have been me. There were connections to be made everywhere. 

Sailing on a ship with a couple thousand folks is a remarkable experience. There are two classes of folks on a cruise ship. The guests are on decks 1-9. Some of those guests think it's a sign of rank how high your cabin is. But then there is the staff. They live on the decks below #1. A, b, b, d, that I know of. 

Servants and served. But for me the overarching experience was that all of us, servant and served alike, we were all on the same ship. 

Perspective provides an ability to see things in 3D. Where before we only saw in black and white. When a different perspective arises, our world can come alive with new perceptions and new insights.

We're all connected. We are stardust.

## Things are amiss

In ancient times a total solar eclipse was a sign from the universe (the gods?), and it had the power to bring down a mighty empire. It could be a powerful enough sign that whole populations might move from one place to another. Immigrants all.

Today it’s a merely a photo-op. Or a missed photo-op as the case may be. Seeing the world from a different perspective allowed me to come home with new insights. When I looked around I saw signs that things were amiss. For me it was more obvious as we traveled around the north Atlantic, following the path that Norse sailors had sailed a thousand years ago. It seemed obvious that things were going badly wrong.

> If we have eyes to see and ears to hear ... But it appears more and more that we don’t have eyes and ears, because we have covered them up.

I could mention a variety of things:

1. melting glaciers
2. Europe burning 
3. droughts and floods
4. people afraid of where their country -- the world -- is headed
5. AI
 
As for myself, anticipating coming home to Palm Harbor, things looked a mess. Some of you may see it differently, and that's ok. I remember fondly the time that I made a similar point in a sermon in Winnsboro, South Carolina. One old gentleman walked up to me after the service and said that he very much disagreed with me. His world was getting better and better every day. I valued his comments more than most because he was thinking about what I had said. That's a victory in today's world.

But I report to you that I **do sense** that things have gone terribly awry in the world around me, and I'm uncertain what to do about it.

## Settings of old narratives

The readings from the Bible that we’ve heard this morning, each of them in their own way, reflect how the world around them had gone awry.

Jonah, such a strange figure, in a strange little book bundled with all the other major and minor prophets. We’re familiar with the major outlines: Jonah was contacted by God with a mission. Jonah said in response, "Is there somebody else up there?" And then he tried to run away. Only to be rescued by God and reluctantly headed off to his assignment, preaching repentance to the Ninevites. They repented and God relented, much to Jonah’s irritation. That’s where we pick it up in today’s reading.  God looks down, sees terrible mischief going on. God assigned someone to warn the offenders, and he is reluctant to follow the voice of God.

Jesus tells a parable to his followers many centuries later. He wants them to become prophetic witnesses to God‘s Kingdom. They are reluctant. 

> Well maybe that’s an ongoing theme?

So in a common pattern, Jesus tells them a story, a parable. It’s a parable about workers and how the manager seems to show no partiality. How might we tell that in today’s world? In a world where workers are generally exploited. In a world where the value of a person is measured in dollars and cents. Mercy and justice are measured in usefulness, and "walking humbly with your God" is but a nice sounding line? 

How do we tell a parable in today’s setting where our expectations of what is appropriate and fair are overthrown by God‘s overwhelming generosity?

If God were to speak to someone in our midst, how would we recognize it? Who could command enough respect from the folks of Palm Harbor in Florida that we would drop everything and try to fulfill our assignment?

## Reluctant Prophets

Given a diagnosis that things are indeed awry, where do we turn? Who do we look to for guidance and direction? Given that we live in a society that is strongly influenced by a sense of “ain’t nobody gonna tell me what to do” Who or where might we find the authority, the motivation to follow God‘s mission?

The old sources of authority seem less and less capable or relevant. Theologies written for a world of centuries ago seem odd living in a world where all of our accumulated knowledge can multiply in days not centuries? The sum total of knowledge that accumulated over centuries, today doubles in a matter of days. The old authorities hold less and less for us. To whom do we turn?

Yes we read from the Bible -- but how many of us make life decisions on the basis that reading? We hear plenty of stories from of old to that effect. But today? 

An old adage puts it: "Want to know where your priorities are? Look at your checkbook."

Of course that adage needs to be reworked since checkbooks are obsolete. But the point is very well taken, "Follow the money."

The Ninevites heard God in Jonah's voice, even though he acted the coward and smelled fishy. Jesus's disciples heard the voice of authority in what today we might call a union organiser.

If God were to speak to one in our midst, how would we recognize it?

## Response

I'm full of questions. Any of you who know me will know that's normal for me. I will make some suggestions in response to my questions. But who am I that you would pay any attention to me?

1. Listen in unexpected ways. Listen in the silence. Listen in the shower. Those are two ways I listen. But I've heard God in clouds. In a bird flying out of a woman's hair. I've seen God in 3 Inuit children in Greenland playing in the water at the beach. (The water had ice flowing in it.) 
2. Pay attention precisely to those who are unlike you. That's why getting outside of our comfort zones is so important. If the message we get sounds too much like ourselves -- we might well have missed it.
3. Respect those who have gone before and honor and protect those who will come after. The past has much to teach us -- but it is often a dead end for us. What we do today is not really for our comfort and pleasure. It is for the generations to come. 
 
> (Shema, yisrael, adonai eloheni, adonai echad. *You shall love the L-rd your [G‑d](https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/433240/jewish/God.htm "God in Judaism") with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might. And these words which I command you today shall be upon your heart. You shall teach them thoroughly to your children, and you shall speak of them when you sit in your house and when you walk on the road, when you lie down and when you rise.*)

I could go on. But it's not about me. 

"Only you can prevent forest fires", Smokey said to us when we were growing up.

Identify where you have heard the prophets among you speaking.

Pay attention.

Bow down in awe.
