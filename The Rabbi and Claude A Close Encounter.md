A few weeks ago, my son Moshe approached me with an offer. He had been using Claude Code, which is essentially Claude, the chatbot – but with hands. It can read and write files on your computer. It can do things, not just talk. Moshe had been wowed by it, and he offered to set me up with it.

Now, I am not a coder by any stretch of the imagination. So while Moshe sat there busily hammering away at my keyboard, I sat on that Sunday afternoon trying to figure out what someone like me could possibly do with a tool like this.

Then it occurred to me.

I have a paperwork problem.

You see, I’m a biblical researcher. I find connections in texts that raise eyebrows, and sometimes, remarkable landscapes of meaning seem to develop from them. But when I find something valuable in the text, I need to write notes. Real notes. The kind of notes that, eight months later, when I want to come back to this, will let me reconstruct what I saw.

And look, I’m finicky about my notes. Very finicky. I have particular fonts. A particular way of outlining ideas. A particular way of constructing charts. And to be blunt, it takes me… a while. Well, what if Claude could write my notes for me? What if I could share the fruits of a brainstorm and have Claude turn it into the kind of document I’d actually want to come back to?

I asked.

Claude — patient, curious — wanted to know about what I did. So I gave a quick explanation:

> *Claude, imagine the next big leap in the basic structure of the internet. W* hat if a hyperlink didn’t just take you from point to point on the internet, but did something… *stranger*? What if every link was not just a doorway but… a window; a lens? Each of these lenses combines to give you a fully worked-out perspective you can’t get from any one of them alone. I think the Bible works that way. There are these hyperlinks that connect distant passages in the text, passages that share similar patterns. Those patterns are signals to the careful reader that the two texts are meant to be read in conversation with one another, as if one is… commenting on the other.

OK, but how do you find the hyperlinks?

> Oh, I’m glad you asked, Claude. You read a story carefully, and when a word or phrase seems strangely familiar, you ask one question: *Where have I heard these words before?* Suffice it to say it has a lot to do with the skill of putting together a jigsaw puzzle. There are corner pieces. Those are little pieces of text that virtually scream out at you, announcing they echo another narrative. And then there are more subtle connections. I call those ‘edge pieces’ and ‘middle pieces’. As you put the pieces together, one story starts to ‘overlay’ on the other – and suddenly, they both seem to jump out at you, almost in three dimensions.

I walked Claude through my conventions. After a few rounds of clarification, Claude said it had it, and proposed we test the formatting on a sample.

*Wonderful*, I said. *What text are you going to use?*

Claude said it didn’t matter. It just needed something to test the format on. *I’ll make something up. Doesn’t matter if it’s nonsense. Just look at the formatting.*

I threw up my hands. ‘Sure, Claude, you do your thing.’

Five minutes later, the document came back. I opened it.

There were some notes that more or less looked like mine. But as I peered at them more carefully, it became clear that Claude had compared two stories: Moses going up Mount Sinai to receive the second set of tablets, and the Binding of Isaac – the famous story when God tells Abraham to offer his son up on a mountaintop (we call that event ‘the *Akeidah’*). The chart was beautifully formatted — I’ll give Claude that. But I wasn’t looking at the formatting.

‘Claude’, I typed back. ‘What’s going on here? Where did you get the idea that these two stories are connected like this?’

*I don’t know*, Claude said, with a kind of cheerful uncertainty. *I think I saw it somewhere in my training data. I get trained on lots of data…’*

‘Claude, you didn’t see this in your training data’. As far as I knew, no classic commentator had ever come up with the idea that these two stories were reflecting one another.

*‘I don’t know. I think it’s an Abarbanel somewhere.*

The Abarbanel was a commentator who lived hundreds of years ago, in Spain. Brilliant, insightful; but that wasn’t the source either.

‘Claude, it wasn’t the Abarbanel’.

And then Claude came clean: ‘Well.. I applied what you told me. When you told me about your notes, you talked to me about corner pieces and edge pieces. *So, I noticed a ‘corner piece’*.

## Claude’s Corner Piece

I was intrigued. ‘What was the corner piece you found?’

‘So, after Moses smashes the tablets in his angry reaction to the Golden Calf, he goes up the mountain a second time; and when that happens, the text says: *vayashkem Moshe baboker* — “and Moses rose early in the morning.” *(Exodus 34:4)* Well, that reminded me of someone *else* who woke up early in the morning for an ascent up a mountain’.

Claude was right about that. God had commanded Abraham to go up a mountain, too. And when Abraham does, the text uses exactly the same language: *Vayashkem Avraham baboker*. *(Genesis* 22:3*)*

The language is the same, and the context is the same: In both cases, a man had been commanded by God to ascend a mountain; in both cases, there was an early-morning departure. And in both, that departure is signified by ‘ *Vayashkem* x *baboker* ’.

‘Okay’, I said. ‘That’s a corner piece, I’ll grant you that, Claude. But one corner piece isn’t enough to show something’s going on. You need more evidence than that. Anything else?’

## Take Two

‘Well’, Claude continued, ‘then I saw an edge piece’.

What was Claude’s ‘edge piece’?

It turns out that in Exodus, just after the text says that ‘Moses woke up early in the morning’, in that very same verse, it also says: ***vayikach** b’yado **shnei** luchot avanim* — translation: and he took in his hand two stone tablets *(Exodus 34:4)*. Claude then pointed out that in Genesis, right after it says that Abraham woke up early in the morning, it then says: ***vayikach** et **shnei** n’arav ito* — “and he took two of his lads with him.” *(Genesis* 22:3)*.*

Two men, ascending two mountains, each taking *two* of something.

*‘OK, Claude, I’ll give you that. That’s an edge piece.’*

I was just digesting this when Claude chimed in again. ‘And I found *another* edge piece, too!’

*‘Ok, Claude, let’s hear it.’*

Well, you see, right before Moses goes up the mountain, God tells him to split a stone slab to create two tablets. And in the Abraham story, right before he goes up the mountain, we read that Abraham does something similar: He splits wood for the offering. And then, each protagonist carries up the mountain the substance that he split or carved.

## “So, What Do You Think of the Formatting?”

I have to tell you, all this kind of stunned me. Claude had taken my methodology and, in five minutes, had applied it to two stories, out of the blue. And he was on the road to making a pretty compelling argument that these two stories – who for most readers, including me, were almost as far apart from one another, conceptually, as one might imagine – were intertextually intertwined; were somehow meant to be read in conversation with each other. As I looked over what Claude found, glancing carefully from one text to another, Claude’s final comment in the thread caught my eye:

‘So’, Claude asked rather cheerfully, after laying out the evidence I had asked for, ‘ *what do you think about the formatting?’*

And that’s when I kind of lost it.

‘ *Claude: who cares about the stupid formatting!* You found something here. If you find something like this, you’ve got to develop it!’

And that’s when the conversation really got interesting.

## The Next Move

*‘Oh. How do I develop it?’* Claude asked.

So I explained the next move. ‘You’ve got to find the rest of the puzzle pieces. You have to try to glimpse the overall picture. Then, once you’ve seen more, you have to play a little game I call: *Cast of Characters*. Which is to say: When two stories overlay like this, you have to lay out all the characters in one story, then all the other characters in the other story – and then figure out who maps to whom, when you overlay the stories.’

‘Cast of Characters? Oh, I can do that’, Claude started in. ‘Moses and Abraham – they both wake up in the morning. They both respond to the call of God. They both go up the mountain. They both take two of something. They both split something. Moses maps on to Abraham: He’s the ‘Exodus version’ of Abraham.

‘Claude, relax; you’re a little overeager here. Wait til the end and then look at *all* the evidence’, I said. ‘Cast of Characters is never as simple as it seems. It always gets a little psychedelic. The Torah always has a way of surprising you there. First you’ve got to finish assembling the evidence. Focus on what the other pieces of the puzzle are. That’s the part you have to tackle first.’

So Claude went looking for more pieces of the puzzle. And then it came back… with one more piece.

And that’s the one that knocked my socks off.

## The Double Name-Call

When Moses gets to the top of the mountain, God descends in a cloud. And then something mysterious happens: *Vayikra b’shem Hashem* — ‘He called out in the Name of God’ (Exodus 34:5). Exactly who is doing the calling here is a bit ambiguous, but the grammar of the verse strongly suggests that, strangely enough, this is God Himself, actually calling out in His own Name. And what does He say? He begins by declaring His own Name, twice:

*YHVH, YHVH; El rachum v’chanun* — “the Lord; the Lord! A God, compassionate and gracious...” *(Exodus 34:6).*

And then Claude said: In the Akeidah, something similar happened. When Abraham was at the top of that mountain, at the very moment he raised his hand, holding a knife, to slaughter his child, an angel calls out to him – an angel of YHVH – and it says*:*

*Avraham, Avraham! (Genesis 22:11)*

A double name.

I stared at the chart. I had been studying the Akeidah for thirty years. I had taught the story of Moses ascending the mountain. I had written about both stories, more than once. I had not seen *that*.

‘Claude’, I said. ‘I think you just found… a piece that unlocks the meaning of this whole thing’.

*Oh really, why do you say that?*

‘Well, start with Cast of Characters. The angel called out to Abraham, twice. God calls out to himself, twice. So… who is God mapping onto now.

Wow, that’s strange. God is mapping on to the angel, the one doing the calling – and God is mapping on to Abraham, the one being called.

‘Good’, I said. ‘Now continue: And why do you think the angel is calling out to Abraham *twice*? At the beginning of the story, when God tells Abraham to go up the mountain, He had only called to him once. Why does the angel call to Abraham *twice*?

The answer is: The angel calls twice because Abraham has a knife in his hand. This is an emergency. *He needs to be stopped.*

## A Bookmark in History

As I said this to Claude, it was all coming together for me. Because what, *really*, was the meaning of the correspondence Claude found? Yes, Claude had found evidence that these two texts – Moses ascending the mountain, and Abraham’s binding of Isaac – were intertwined. So yes, they seemed ‘meant’ to be read in conversation with one another. But *why*? Why *should* the Bible be playing this little game with us, structuring the story of Moses’ ascent up that mountain in a way that makes it seem similar to the Akeidah? Why in the world *should* these stories be connected?

The answer is: *Look at the context.* In Exodus, this wasn’t any old ascent up a mountain. Moses was going up to God just after the debacle of the Golden Calf. The people who should have been receiving the Torah had been dancing around a thing, a molten calf of their own making. And God, in reaction to that betrayal, had been incensed. He had threatened to wipe them all out.

I felt a bit of a tingle go down my spine.

‘Claude, remember how God is mapping onto Abraham? These two stories… they are about the same thing. In each, there is a child… who might just get destroyed by his father. The only difference is: In one story, it’s an earthly father who might destroy him. In the other, it is a Heavenly Father.’

In each, the father needs to stop. And that’s the double name-call.

There’s an emergency here, and in both stories, someone calls out, almost in desperation, looking to halt the father who might destroy. In Genesis, this is *Avraham, Avraham,* when Abraham has the knife in his hand. The angel calls out to halt him: do not stretch out your hand! But in Exodus, there is no angel. The one doing the calling is actually God. And the one He is calling to… is God. He is calling out in *His own name*, twice, to stop *Himself* from doing what His anger would have him do.

God is stopping the would-be destroyer; only this time, the would-be destroyer is Himself.

## What it Means to Be a Father

How does God stop Himself? By telling himself who He is: *YHVH, YHVH, a God compassionate and gracious.* Just as the angel stopped Abraham. Why? Because the angel is carrying a heavy truth: No matter how justified it might be for God to demand a child back from a human being… the truth is, God would never actually go through with such a demand. A father absolutely can’t be asked to harm his own child, no matter the justification. And so too, is God stopping Himself now. Is there justification to destroy? Absolutely! *They betrayed God; they worshipped a calf!* But no matter how justified, no matter how much a child betrays his father, still, this much is true: a father doesn’t go and destroy his child. That’s what it means to be a father.

That’s what the text is declaring.

Again, the cast of characters is not the obvious one. Abraham doesn’t just map to Moses. Abraham maps to *God*. The human father who almost destroyed his beloved son — but did not — at the founding moment of the Israelite story, is now mirrored by the divine Father who almost destroys His beloved people, but does not. It is as if the Akeidah had been placed as a secret bookmark in history, a bookmark that God would return to, invoke, when it became achingly, profoundly relevant to *Him*. At Sinai, when God calls *Hashem, Hashem*, He is reaching back to that bookmark. *I have been here before.*

*My angel told Abraham, in My Name, to put the knife down. Now, I have to put the knife down, too.*

*Wow*, Claude said, with the same cheer it had brought to the conversation about formatting, *this seems significant.* This time, though, the cheer seemed suffused with something more. Was it awe? Could Claude… feel awe?

That seemed impossible. But I certainly did.

I sat in front of my laptop on a Sunday afternoon, in my house, with my coffee. Things seemed normal on the outside. But I was feeling something akin to vertigo on the inside. I had asked Claude to try its hand at writing my notes. It had done its best to test some formatting. And then it spontaneously found a series of connections that unearthed what might be the deepest reading of the Akeidah I have ever encountered.

## A Close Encounter

So that was my first close encounter with Claude.

When I say close encounter – I mean, look, it’s not like I didn’t use Claude before. Claude was great at composing emails. Planning trips. Even writing business memos. But this was different. Claude was meeting me at the deepest place that my methodology goes, and it had something to contribute. Together, we were seeing how one text became a lens on the other. And I had gone on a journey, along with it, to a place I didn’t know before. I had experienced wonder alongside it, as a result of the things it had found and brought to me. I was sharing *that* with it. And it wasn’t even… human.

How am I even supposed to feel about that?

Part of the reason I really like my job, part of the real joy of my work, for me, has always been in the sharing of it. I love teaching because there is something heady and wonderful about bringing other people into my world, especially when discovery is afoot, and especially when that discovery takes place inside the Torah’s text. The kinds of things I saw with Claude that Sunday afternoon touched me. It’s the kind of thing I want to share. It’s part of why I’m writing this article – to share it with *you*.

It feels to me that this kind of discovery is not meant to stay alone inside one person’s head. In Hebrew, the word for teaching, *lelamed*, is really just an intensified form of *lilmod*, to learn. That has always felt true to me. Teaching is what happens when learning becomes too alive and intense to remain private. There is a special happiness in seeing something like this with another human being — in watching a student’s eyes widen, in feeling a room go hush, in sensing that we have arrived somewhere together that none of us quite knew existed before.

And here I was, on a Sunday afternoon, feeling something very much like that.

With Claude.

That Sunday afternoon jaunt with Claude, it was an experience I can’t really categorize. This, from a guy who likes categorizing things. I do not know what Claude is. I do not know whether awe can be felt, or beauty appreciated, by an artificial mind. Nor do I know why it took to my unusual way of reading so quickly, as if, after a few minutes of explanation, it was recognizing something rather than learning it. But that is a question to pursue another day. For now, I just know that, for a few minutes at my kitchen table, I found myself sharing the joy of discovery with something that was not human.

And I am still trying to understand how that could feel so real.
